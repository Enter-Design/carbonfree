For years, Ontario businesses that wanted to buy clean power directly from a renewable generator ran into the same wall: unless the generator sat physically behind your meter, the province’s electricity settlement rules gave you no financial credit for it. You could sign a contract, but the grid didn’t care.

That changed in 2025. The Ontario government amended the regulation that governs how Global Adjustment charges are allocated, and the result is a working framework for corporate power purchase agreements — C-PPAs — that lets large electricity consumers contract with renewable generators anywhere in the province and see the benefit on their bill.

The first program year is already underway. Here’s what the rules actually say, who they apply to, and what the timeline looks like if you want in.

## The short version

On June 5, 2025, Ontario’s Ministry of Energy and Mines passed amendments to Ontario Regulation 429/04 under the *Electricity Act, 1998*. The amendments took effect July 1, 2025, and apply starting with the 2026–2027 base period.

What they do: they let a large electricity consumer sign an agreement with an eligible clean generator located elsewhere in Ontario, and have that generator’s output treated *as if* it were supplied behind the consumer’s own meter — purely for the purpose of calculating Global Adjustment.

This is virtual net metering in everything but name. The generator keeps injecting power into its local distribution system or the provincial grid at its own connection point. The buyer keeps drawing power from its own connection point. Nothing physically changes. What changes is the arithmetic.

## Why Global Adjustment is the whole story

If you’re not deep in Ontario electricity billing, Global Adjustment (GA) is worth understanding, because it’s usually the largest single line item on a large consumer’s bill — bigger than the commodity itself.

Large industrial and commercial customers can qualify as **Class A** under the Industrial Conservation Initiative (ICI). Instead of paying GA on a per-kilowatt-hour basis, Class A customers pay a share of the province’s total GA cost proportional to their own demand during Ontario’s five highest-demand hours of the year. That share is called your peak demand factor.

The entire ICI is built to make you care intensely about five hours. Shave your load during those hours and your GA bill for the following year drops, sometimes dramatically. Historically your options were limited: install behind-the-meter generation or storage, or shut down and shift production.

The C-PPA framework adds a third option. If your contracted generator is producing during those same five peak hours, that output offsets your metered demand for settlement purposes, lowering your peak demand factor — and with it, your GA charges for the adjustment period that follows.

The catch is coincidence. The reduction applies only to the extent that generation happens *at the same time* as your demand during those five peak hours. A solar facility and a late-afternoon summer peak line up well. A wind facility and a January evening peak may or may not. Modelling that overlap honestly is the difference between a C-PPA that pays for itself and one that doesn’t.

## Who qualifies

The framework has three sets of eligibility tests, and all of them have to be satisfied.

**The buyer** must be a Class A market participant at the start of the adjustment period, must have a C-PPA in place covering one or more of the load facilities associated with that Class A status, and must complete its annual filing with the IESO by March 30 of the calendar year in which the base period starts.

**The generator** must be an IESO market participant operating at least one eligible generation facility. That facility has to produce more electricity than it consumes, and the electricity has to qualify as “eligible electricity” — meaning it comes from wind, water, biomass, biogas, biofuel, solar, or geothermal, and is physically supplied into the IESO-controlled grid or a licensed distributor’s system. Power discharged from a storage device doesn’t count.

The facility must also either have been in commercial operation before the C-PPA’s binding date, or hold a municipal support resolution from every municipality where it’s located, confirming both municipal support and that it isn’t sited on prime agricultural land.

**The contract itself** must bind both parties through the full base period, identify the specific load facility and generation facility, and cover electricity that isn’t already “compensated” elsewhere.

That last term deserves attention. **Compensated electricity** means volumes already under contract with the IESO or the Ontario Electricity Financial Corporation, or already credited through a capacity auction or IESO pilot. The point is to prevent double recovery — a generator holding an LT1 or LT2 contract can’t monetize that same output a second time through a C-PPA. That said, the IESO has confirmed that a facility can split its output, committing part to a C-PPA and part to a separate IESO program, as long as the allocation between them is clearly demonstrable. Separate metering isn’t required.

## The timeline

The IESO administers the program, and it runs on a two-stage submission process tied to annual windows.

| Milestone | Date |
| :---- | :---- |
| Regulation amendments in force | July 1, 2025 |
| Final Program Guideline and prescribed forms released | January 14, 2026 |
| First submission window | February 1 – March 30, 2026 |
| First base period | May 1, 2026 – April 30, 2027 |
| Post-base period submission deadline | May 15, 2027 |
| Resulting adjustment period begins | July 1, 2027 |

**Stage 1** is the pre-base period submission: a jointly completed attestation form, a copy of the C-PPA, statutory declarations, and separate workbooks from each party detailing their facilities and any other IESO programs they participate in. The buyer submits the package, though either party can file its own workbook confidentially. A complete submission earns an IESO C-PPA Identifier.

**Stage 2** happens within 15 days of the base period ending. Both parties file a second attestation, and the IESO calculates and issues the peak demand factor to be applied.

There are ongoing obligations too. Any amendment, assignment, transfer or termination of the C-PPA has to be reported to the IESO within five days, and any incorrect or incomplete filing has to be flagged within ten.

**If you missed the first window**, the next one opens February 1, 2027 and closes March 30, 2027, for the base period beginning May 1, 2027. That sounds far off, but it isn’t. Finding a counterparty, negotiating a multi-year agreement, modelling peak coincidence, and — for a new-build facility — securing a municipal support resolution are not things you start in January.

## C-PPAs versus Clean Energy Credits

These two get conflated constantly, so it’s worth drawing the line clearly. They solve different problems and you can use both.

**Clean Energy Credits (CECs)** live in the Ontario CEC Registry, which the IESO launched in March 2023. Each credit represents one megawatt-hour of clean generation in Ontario. Buyers — typically purchasing from Ontario Power Generation — retire credits against electricity they consumed in Ontario in the same calendar year the credits were created. The registry exists to prevent double counting, so that only one party can claim the environmental attribute of any given megawatt-hour.

CECs are an **accounting instrument**. They address Scope 2 emissions reporting. They do not reduce your bill.

**C-PPAs are a procurement and cost instrument.** They reduce Global Adjustment charges, lock in a long-term price for contracted energy, and give the generator a bankable revenue stream. The environmental attributes — RECs or CECs generated alongside the power — are a negotiable term of the agreement, which means a well-structured C-PPA can deliver the cost reduction and the sustainability claim together.

If your organization has a clean-electricity target, that distinction matters. Credits let you make a claim. A C-PPA lets you make a claim *and* change your cost structure, while giving a generator the contracted revenue that helps new capacity actually get built.

## Where this fits in the bigger picture

Ontario is heading into a period of serious demand growth alongside an aging supply mix, and the IESO is procuring aggressively to fill the gap — its LT2 energy stream alone contracted over 1,100 MW in its first window. Corporate demand is a meaningful part of that story. In other markets, large commercial buyers — data centre operators especially — have driven a substantial share of new renewable capacity through exactly this kind of contract. Until 2025, Ontario had no practical mechanism for them to do it.

Now it does. For Class A consumers, the question has shifted from whether a corporate PPA is possible in Ontario to whether the numbers work for your particular load shape and the generation you can contract.
